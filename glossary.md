## Week 1 — Human Visual System
*(full explanations in [`week1-study-notes.md`](./week1-study-notes.md); this is the lookup-speed version)*

- **[Aperture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aperture)** — camera terminology for the opening that controls how much light gets let in (a wider opening = more light); the pupil is the eye's own aperture. Deepened in Week 2.
- **[Image sensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_sensor)** — the electronic chip behind a camera's lens that converts incoming light into a digital image, sitting where photographic film used to go; the retina plays the same biological role. Deepened in Week 2.
- **[Bayer color filter array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayer_filter) / RGGB mosaic** — the physical grid of tiny red/green/green/blue filters glued onto a sensor, one per pixel, so each pixel measures only one color; the rest gets computationally filled in later ([demosaicking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosaicing), Week 3).
- **[dpi (dots per inch)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dots_per_inch)** — pixel *size* flipped into pixel *density*: if a pixel is p inches wide, dpi = 1/p. Historically a printing term ([ppi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel_density) is the more precise word for screens), but used interchangeably with pixel density here (§9).
- **[Accommodation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accommodation_(vertebrate_eye))** — the eye changing focus by reshaping its lens via the ciliary muscle.
- **[Fovea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fovea_centralis)** — the small, cone-dense pit in the retina responsible for sharp central vision.
- **[Rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rod_cell) / [cones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cone_cell)** — the retina's two photoreceptor types: rods for low-light/no-color vision, cones for color and fine detail.
- **[Metamerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamerism_(color))** — two physically different light spectra producing identical cone responses, so they look like the same color.
- **Visual angle / [arcminute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minute_and_second_of_arc)** — the angular size an object subtends at the eye (1° = 60 arcmin); acuity and spatial frequency are both defined in these units, not raw distance.
- **[Dynamic range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_range)** — the ratio between the brightest and darkest signal a system (eye, sensor, or display) can represent, in f-stops or orders of magnitude.
- **[Weber contrast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrast_(vision)#Weber_contrast) / [Michelson contrast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrast_(vision)#Michelson_contrast)** — two different formulas for "contrast": Weber for one feature against a uniform background, Michelson for a repeating pattern.
- **[Spatial frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_frequency)** — how rapidly *brightness* varies across an image, in cycles per unit distance (or cycles per degree, perceptually); not to be confused with the frequency of light itself (color, §3) or temporal frequency (flicker/frame rate, §17). Full buildup, including why the same pattern's cycles-per-degree changes with viewing distance, in §12.
- **Image frequency** — how rapidly brightness varies across a digital image's *pixel grid* (cycles per pixel), a fixed property of the file alone, independent of print size, display, or viewing distance. Converts into physical spatial frequency via dpi, and from there into cycles-per-degree via viewing distance — the full chain, worked numerically, is in §12.2.1.
- **[Contrast Sensitivity Function (CSF)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contrast_(vision)#Contrast_sensitivity)** — the curve describing how sensitive the eye is to a given spatial frequency; band-pass, peaking around 4–6 cycles per degree.
- **[Low-pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-pass_filter) / [high-pass filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-pass_filter)** — operations that keep only coarse/smooth content, or only fine detail/edges, respectively.
- **[Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_transform) (1D → 2D)** — the operation that rewrites a signal (a 1D signal like sound, or a 2D signal like an image) as a sum of sine waves of different frequency, amplitude, and phase; for images, the "wave" is a 2D sinusoidal grating with a horizontal frequency *u* and vertical frequency *v*. Introduced early, in §12.4, because HW1's hybrid-images task depends on it; formalized fully in Week 5.
- **Spatial domain / frequency domain** — two equally complete descriptions of the same image: spatial domain indexes by pixel location (ordinary "what color is here"); frequency domain indexes by `(u,v)` spatial-frequency pair (how much of that stripe pattern is present). The Fourier transform converts between them; nothing is lost (§12.4.0).
- **Spectrum (magnitude / phase)** — the Fourier transform's output, one complex number per `(u,v)` pair: magnitude = how strong that frequency is, phase = how it's shifted. Low `(u,v)` = coarse image content, high `(u,v)` = fine detail (§12.4.3).
- **DC component** — the `(u,v) = (0,0)` entry of a spectrum: zero spatial frequency, equal to the image's overall average brightness (§12.4.4).
- **[fftshift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_Fourier_transform) / ifftshift** — reorders a raw FFT output so the DC component moves from a corner to the center (`fftshift`), or undoes that reordering before an inverse transform (`ifftshift`) — needed because of how the discrete Fourier transform indexes negative frequencies (§12.4.4).
- **[Convolution theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolution_theorem)** — multiplying two spectra in the frequency domain is mathematically identical to convolving (a sliding weighted average of) the corresponding signals in the spatial domain; the mechanism behind frequency-domain filtering (§12.4.5) and, later, the PSF and deconvolution (Weeks 5–6).
- **[Stereoscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stereoscopy)** — literally "two-eyed viewing" (*stereo* + *scopic*); describes any device or display that feeds each eye a slightly different image (mimicking binocular disparity) so the brain perceives depth from an otherwise flat picture.
- **[Vergence-accommodation conflict (VAC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vergence-accommodation_conflict)** — the mismatch, in stereo displays, between where the eyes converge (simulated depth) and where they must focus (actual screen distance).
- **[Inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product_space) (dot product)** — multiply two vectors entry by entry and add up; a cone's response is the inner product of the sampled spectrum with that cone's sampled sensitivity curve, and each Fourier coefficient is the inner product of a signal with one sinusoid (§3, §12.4.1).
- **[Linear map / linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_map)** — a function that preserves sums and scaling, f(a·x + b·y) = a·f(x) + b·f(y), so it can be written as a matrix; the eye's spectrum-to-(L, M, S) map, the Bayer mosaic, and convolution are all examples (§3, §4, §12.4.5).
- **[Change of basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_of_basis)** — rewriting the same vector's coordinates relative to a different set of basis vectors, via an invertible matrix; the DFT is a change of basis from spikes to sinusoids, and color spaces (XYZ, RGB) are changes of basis for (L, M, S) (§3, §12.4.1).
- **[Null space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_(linear_algebra))** — every input a matrix sends to zero, i.e. every change it cannot see; metamers differ by a null-space vector of the cone matrix, and the Bayer mosaic's null space is why demosaicking can't just invert it (§3, §4).
- **[Projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_(linear_algebra))** — a linear map P with P² = P that keeps one subspace and discards the rest; a 0/1 frequency mask is a projection onto the kept sinusoids, and I − P onto the others (§12.4.5, §13.2).
- **[Circulant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circulant_matrix) / [Toeplitz matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toeplitz_matrix)** — a matrix whose every row is the row above shifted by one (with wrap-around for circulant; constant along each diagonal for Toeplitz); the matrix form of convolution with a fixed kernel (§12.4.5).
- **[Diagonalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonalizable_matrix) / eigenbasis** — choosing a basis of [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors) (vectors a matrix only scales) so the matrix becomes diagonal; the Fourier basis diagonalizes every circulant matrix, with the kernel's DFT as eigenvalues, which is the convolution theorem (§12.4.5).

## Week 2 — Digital Photography I (ray optics, aperture, sensor)
*(full explanations in [`week2-study-notes.md`](./week2-study-notes.md); this is the lookup-speed version)*

- **[Ray optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometrical_optics)** — modeling light as straight-line rays, ignoring wave effects — the assumption behind pinhole-camera geometry (HW1) and the thin lens model (§0, §4).
- **Pinhole camera / [camera obscura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura)** — a barrier with a single small opening that lets only one ray per scene point reach the sensor, forming an inverted, scaled image (§1).
- **[Aperture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aperture)** — the opening controlling how much light enters a lens or pinhole; analogous to the iris/pupil. Deepened here with the f-number/stops formalism (§8).
- **Focal length (f)** — the distance from a pinhole or lens to the plane where a sharp image forms; an **intrinsic** property fixed at manufacture, not something a photographer adjusts by moving anything (§1, §4.2).
- **Object distance (S) vs. sensor distance (S′)** — S is how far the actual subject sits from the lens, a property of the *scene*; S′ is how far the lens sits from the sensor, a property of the *setup*, adjusted by the focus ring. The thin lens equation ties the two to the (intrinsic) focal length; "focusing" means physically changing S′ until it matches what the equation demands for the S you want sharp (§4.1–4.3).
- **Magnification (m)** — the ratio of image height to object height produced by a lens, m = (S′ − f)/f = S′/S (§4.1).
- **[Refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction)** — the bending of light at a boundary between two materials of different optical density; the mechanism a lens uses to redirect rays (§4).
- **Thin lens model / Gaussian lens formula** — a simplified lens model (zero thickness) built on two assumptions (center rays pass straight through; parallel rays converge at the focal plane), giving the thin lens equation 1/S + 1/S′ = 1/f, derived from two similar-triangle ray relations (§4.1).
- **Defocus / actual object distance (O)** — a scene point at actual distance O ≠ the currently-focused distance S has rays that converge before or after the sensor plane, landing as a blurred circle of confusion instead of a sharp point; never happens with an ideal pinhole (§5).
- **Aberrations** (spherical, [chromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_aberration), oblique/coma/distortion) — systematic deviations from ideal thin-lens focusing, caused respectively by spherical (not hyperbolic) lens shapes, wavelength-dependent refraction (dispersion), and off-axis geometry (§6).
- **[Field of view (FOV)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_of_view)** — the angular extent of scene captured, set by focal length and sensor size; the camera analog of Week 1's eye FOV (§7).
- **[f-number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-number)** — ratio of focal length to aperture diameter (N = f/D); smaller means more light and shallower depth of field (§8).
- **Stop** — a change in light by a factor of 2×, the standard unit for spacing aperture (and exposure) settings; the same unit as Week 1's dynamic-range f-stops (§8).
- **[Circle of confusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_confusion)** — the blur disc a defocused point forms on the sensor, c = m·D·|O − S|/O, derived from two similar-triangle relations tying the aperture, the focused distance S, and the actual distance O (§9.2).
- **[Depth of field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depth_of_field)** — the range of actual object distances whose circle of confusion stays below an acceptable (pixel-set) threshold ε, DOF ≈ 2εS/(mD) — centered on the focused distance S, not a free "actual distance"; the range's exact edges are its near and far distances, O_near = S/(1+k) and O_far = S/(1-k) with k = ε/(mD) (§9.3, §9.3.1).
- **[Pixel pitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel_pitch)** — the center-to-center spacing of sensor pixels, a length (sensor width ÷ pixel count across it, typically a few µm); converts a blur threshold stated in pixels into the length the circle-of-confusion and depth-of-field formulas need (§9.3).
- **Hyperfocal distance (H)** — the focus distance that extends the far edge of the depth of field to infinity, H = f²/(Nc), where this c is the fixed acceptable-blur threshold, not the general circle-of-confusion variable (§9.4); focusing at H makes the near edge of the depth of field exactly H/2 (evaluating §9.3's near-distance formula at k=1).
- **Diffraction limit / [numerical aperture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_aperture) (NA)** — the smallest resolvable spot size set by diffraction alone (Abbe's formula, d ≈ λ/(2·NA) ≈ λN); trades off against depth of field via f-number (§2, §10).
- **Optimal pinhole diameter** — the pinhole size that minimizes total image blur by balancing geometric blur (grows with diameter *d*) against diffraction spread (shrinks with *d*): d = 2√(fλ), where *f* is the pinhole-to-image-plane distance and λ is the wavelength of light (§2.1).
- **[Exposure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposure_(photography))** — total light collected per unit sensor area, H = E·t ∝ L·t/N², set by exposure time and aperture (and scene brightness); ISO scales the recorded image's brightness but does not change H (§13.1–13.2).
- **Exposure time / shutter speed** — how long the shutter lets each pixel collect light (e.g. 1/500 s, 2 s, "bulb"); a "fast shutter speed" is a short exposure time (§13.1).
- **[Shutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shutter_(photography))** — the mechanical curtain or electronic reset/readout that starts and stops light collection (§13.1).
- **Equivalent exposures / reciprocity** — (time, aperture) pairs with the same t/N² deliver the same exposure; they differ in motion blur and depth of field instead of brightness (§13.2–13.3).
- **[Exposure value (EV)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposure_value)** — EV = log₂(N²/t), a single number labeling a family of equivalent exposures; one EV = one stop (§13.3).
- **Full-well capacity / saturation ([clipping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clipping_(photography)))** — the maximum charge a pixel can hold; overexposed pixels hit it and all read the same maximum, losing detail irrecoverably (§13.4).
- **Underexposure / overexposure** — too little light (signal buried in the noise floor) vs. too much (highlights saturate); correct exposure fits the scene inside the sensor's dynamic range (§13.4).
- **[Motion blur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_blur)** — streaking from scene or camera motion during the exposure; streak length = image-plane speed × exposure time; mathematically a convolution with a box kernel (§13.5).
- **[Burst photography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burst_mode_(photography))** — capturing many short exposures and merging them, avoiding blur at the cost of paying read noise once per frame (§13.5).
- **Active vs. passive sensing / range gating** — a passive camera collects existing light; an active sensor (LiDAR, ToF camera) supplies its own; range gating opens the detector only during a nanosecond window when the laser echo can arrive, an ultra-short exposure that rejects ambient light (§13.8).
- **Integration time (ToF)** — a time-of-flight camera's name for exposure time: longer gives less noisy depth but more motion artifacts and saturation (§13.8).
- **Sensor** — the chip converting incident light into an electrical signal — the camera's "retina" (§11).
- **[Photoelectric effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photoelectric_effect)** — a photon striking a photodiode knocking loose an electron; the basic photon-to-signal conversion inside every pixel (§11).
- **[Quantum efficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_efficiency) / fill factor** — the fraction of incoming photons actually converted to signal, and the fraction of a pixel's area that is light-sensitive, respectively (§11).
- **[CCD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge-coupled_device) / [CMOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CMOS_sensor)** — two sensor architectures for converting and reading out pixel charge; CCD trades speed/cost for sensitivity/noise, CMOS the reverse (§12).
- **[ISO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_speed)** — a sensor gain setting, usually applied before the ADC, that amplifies signal (and noise) without collecting more light (§13).
- **Bit depth** — the number of discrete digital levels a sensor's ADC can output (12–14 bits RAW vs. 8 bits JPEG), an additional cap on dynamic range beyond the physical noise floor (§14).
- **[Rolling shutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_shutter) / global shutter** — row-by-row vs. all-at-once pixel exposure timing; rolling shutter can produce motion/flicker banding (e.g. 120 Hz AC-light flicker) but can also be exploited as a fine-grained temporal sensor (§15).
- **[Gaussian noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_noise)** — additive, signal-independent sensor noise from thermal/read/amplifier sources (§16).
- **[Shot noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot_noise) / [read noise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_noise#Read_noise)** — shot noise: fundamental, signal-dependent randomness in photon arrival counts (Poisson-distributed, standard deviation = √N). Read noise: added by the sensor's own readout electronics, signal-independent (§16).
- **[Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal-to-noise_ratio)** — mean signal divided by its standard deviation; combines shot noise, dark current, and read noise into one formula (§16).
- **[Homogeneous coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_coordinates)** — appending a 1 to a point, (X, Y, Z, 1), so perspective projection becomes a linear matrix product followed by one division by depth; the pinhole's 3×4 camera matrix (§1).
- **[Rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank_(linear_algebra))** — the number of independent directions a matrix's output can span; the pinhole camera matrix has rank 3 (depth is lost), and a 4×4 Bayer patch's 16×48 selection matrix has rank 16 (§1, §11).
- **[Condition number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condition_number)** — largest ÷ smallest [singular value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_value) of a matrix: how much measurement errors can be amplified when inverting it; about 2.0 × 10⁶ for a toy bare sensor, exactly 1 for an ideal pinhole (§0).
- **[Ray transfer (ABCD) matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_transfer_matrix_analysis)** — a 2×2 matrix acting on a ray's (height, angle): travel by L is [[1, L], [0, 1]], a thin lens is [[1, 0], [−1/f, 1]], and the thin lens equation is the condition that their product's top-right entry is 0 (§4.1).
- **[Underdetermined system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underdetermined_system)** — fewer equations (measurements) than unknowns, so infinitely many solutions fit the data; the Bayer mosaic (16 measurements of 48 unknowns in a 4×4 patch) is one, resolved only by adding assumptions (§11).
- **Selection matrix** — a matrix whose every row holds a single 1 and zeros elsewhere, picking out one entry of the input; the color filter array written as a matrix (§11).

## Week 3 — Digital Photography II (color science, ISP, demosaicking, denoising)
*(full explanations in [`week3-study-notes.md`](./week3-study-notes.md); this is the lookup-speed version)*

- **[Spectral sensitivity function (SSF)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_sensitivity)** — a sensor's (eye cone or camera pixel's) per-wavelength responsiveness; its response to incident light is the integral of the light's spectral power distribution weighted by this function, R = ∫Φ(λ)f(λ)dλ (§1).
- **Retinal (tristimulus) color space / [metamerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metamerism_(color))** — the 3D space of achievable (S,M,L) cone responses; monochromatic light traces a "lasso curve" confined to the positive octant, and varying its strength sweeps out a convex cone with a horseshoe-shaped cross-section. Metamers (Week 1 §3) are physically different spectra landing on the same interior point of this cone (§2).
- **[CIE color matching functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIE_1931_color_space#Color_matching)** — the weights of three fixed primaries needed to visually match each pure spectral color; some matches require a negative coefficient, recorded when a primary had to be added to the test side instead of the primary side (§3).
- **[Standard observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIE_1931_color_space#CIE_standard_observer)** — an idealized "average" viewer built by pooling color-matching data from a small panel of real observers; formally it *is* its three color matching functions, and every CIE number describes what this averaged viewer would report (§3).
- **[Luminous efficiency function V(λ)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminous_efficiency_function)** — the eye's relative brightness sensitivity at each wavelength (peak normalized to 1, in the green); CIE XYZ's Y coordinate was defined so its matching function equals V(λ), which makes Y luminance (§4).
- **[CIE XYZ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIE_1931_color_space) / CIE RGB** — two equivalent tristimulus bases for color: CIE RGB uses physically realizable primaries but needs negative coordinates for some colors; CIE XYZ guarantees non-negative coordinates for every color but its "primaries" aren't physically realizable light. No basis can have both (§4).
- **[CIE xy chromaticity diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIE_1931_color_space#Chromaticity_diagram)** — a 2D projection of 3D CIE XYZ (x=X/(X+Y+Z), y=Y/(X+Y+Z)) that discards luminance and keeps only hue/saturation (§5).
- **[Color gamut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamut)** — the set of colors a device can reproduce; for any 3 real primaries this is exactly the triangle of their chromaticity coordinates (a convex combination of 3 points), e.g. the sRGB gamut. Points outside the triangle need a negative coordinate and are unreproducible by that primary set (§6–§7).
- **[Display P3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DCI-P3)** — a wide-gamut RGB standard used by many phone/laptop screens: the cinema DCI-P3 primaries paired with a D65 white; the same RGB numbers mean a different color in Display P3 than in sRGB (§7).
- **[Image signal processor (ISP) pipeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processor)** — the stage sequence turning a RAW Bayer image into a finished photo: demosaicking → denoising → gamut mapping → gamma correction → compression, plus dead-pixel removal, dark-frame subtraction, lens correction, and sharpening (§9).
- **[Exif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exif)** — capture metadata (exposure, aperture, ISO, timestamp, lens) stored alongside an image's pixel data (§9).
- **[RAW file](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raw_image_format) / [dcraw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dcraw)** — a camera's container for its nearly unprocessed sensor mosaic plus the metadata needed to interpret it (filter pattern, black/white levels, white-balance gains, color matrix); dcraw is the classic command-line decoder, and rawpy the Python wrapper around its successor library, LibRaw (§9.1).
- **Black level / white level** — the fixed electronic offset a no-light pixel reads (subtracted first) and the saturated maximum (divided by, after subtraction) that together put RAW values on a [0, 1] linear scale (§9.1).
- **[White balance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_balance)** — per-channel gains that make a neutral gray object come out with R = G = B, removing the light source's color cast; a diagonal matrix acting on (R, G, B) (§9.1).
- **[Demosaicking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosaicing)** — reconstructing full color from a Bayer sensor where each pixel measured only one channel; naive per-channel averaging (§10.1) improves through chrominance low-pass filtering (§10.3) and edge-directed methods (§10.4) to Malvar-He-Cutler (§10.5).
- **[Optical low-pass filter (OLPF)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_low-pass_filter)** — a birefringent glass layer in front of a sensor that pre-blurs incoming light to soften Bayer-sampling aliasing, at the cost of resolution (§10.2).
- **[Y′CbCr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YCbCr)** — a luma (Y′) + chrominance (Cb, Cr) color representation, related to RGB by a fixed matrix and offset; used to filter only chrominance during demosaicking (§10.3) and to subsample only chrominance in JPEG (§14), since human vision is far more sensitive to luminance sharpness.
- **[Chroma subsampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chroma_subsampling)** — storing the chroma channels (Cb, Cr) at lower spatial resolution than luma (Y′), e.g. JPEG's 4:2:2 and 4:2:0, because the eye resolves fine color detail far less well than fine brightness detail (§10.3.1, §14).
- **Malvar-He-Cutler demosaicking** — a linear demosaicking filter that adds a gain times a same-location, cross-channel gradient (Laplacian) correction to the naive interpolation estimate; published gains α=1/2, β=5/8, γ=3/4 (§10.5).
- **[PSNR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_signal-to-noise_ratio) / MSE** — mean squared error (averaged over pixels and color channels) and its log-scale, max-value-normalized ratio, PSNR=10·log₁₀(max²/MSE); the metric HW2 uses to numerically compare demosaicking methods (§10.6).
- **[Denoising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noise_reduction)** — computationally removing sensor noise after capture; most methods specialize one weighted-average framework, i_denoised(x) = (1/normalizer)·Σw(x,x')·i_noisy(x'), differing only in how the weight w is defined (§11.1).
- **[Bilateral filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilateral_filter)** (Tomasi & Manduchi, 1998) — a denoising filter weighting by the product of a spatial Gaussian and an intensity-difference Gaussian, so it smooths noise within a region but not across an edge ("edge-aware smoothing") (§11.4).
- **[Non-local means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-local_means)** (Buades, Coll & Morel, 2005) — a denoising filter weighting by patch similarity rather than single-pixel intensity, searching a bounded window and exploiting image self-similarity regardless of spatial distance (§11.5).
- **NLM filtering parameter *h* / normalizer *Z(i)*** — HW2's symbols for non-local means: *h* sets how different two patches may be before their weight collapses (*h*² = 2σ² in these notes' form), and *Z(i)* is the sum of the weights, dividing by which makes them a true weighted average (§11.5).
- **[BM3D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block-matching_and_3D_filtering)** — a state-of-the-art (not HW2-required) denoising method: group similar patches into 3D blocks and collaboratively filter each block via a DCT transform, coefficient thresholding, and inverse transform (§11.7).
- **[Unsharp masking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unsharp_masking)** — sharpening by adding back an amplified detail layer: sharpened = I + k·(I − blur(I)), with strength k chosen by the user (§11.8).
- **Detail layer** — I − blur(I): the high image-frequency part of an image (edges and texture) that a blur removed; near zero in smooth regions (§11.8).
- **Halo artifact** — the bright/dark outline hugging a strong edge after sharpening, caused by overshoot and undershoot when the edge itself lands in the detail layer (e.g. with a Gaussian blur); an edge-preserving bilateral blur largely avoids it (§11.8).
- **[Gamma correction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma_correction) / [sRGB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SRGB)** — encoding linear sensor intensity with a power-law-like curve so finite bit-depth code steps match perceived (not physical) brightness steps; PS2's simplified form is I→I^(1/2.2), while the sRGB standard uses an exact piecewise linear/power-law formula (threshold 0.0031308, exponent 1/2.4, constant α=0.055) (§12).
- **Gamut mapping** — converting a camera's native color gamut to a standard gamut (typically sRGB) via a camera XYZ → CIE XYZ → sRGB chain (§13).
- **Gamut clipping** — the simplest gamut-mapping strategy: clamp each linear RGB channel of an out-of-gamut color to [0, 1] independently; cheap, but shifts hue/brightness and flattens saturated gradations into one patch (§13).
- **Gamut compression** — a smoother gamut-mapping strategy that moves out-of-gamut colors inward (e.g. desaturating toward a gray of the same luminance), often nudging nearby in-gamut colors too so shades stay distinguishable; one source of camera "modes" (§13).
- **[JPEG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JPEG) / [DCT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_cosine_transform)** — the standard lossy image compression pipeline: RGB→Y′CbCr, chroma subsampling (4:4:4/4:2:2/4:2:0), 8×8-block discrete cosine transform, coefficient quantization, and entropy/run-length coding (§14).
- **[Deconvolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deconvolution)** — computationally undoing a known blur; Week 3 only previews this with one example slide (blur sources: defocus, geometric distortion, spherical/chromatic aberration, coma) and no formula — full treatment, and the inverse-problem framing, is Week 5–6's subject.
- **[Conic hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conical_combination) / [convex hull](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_hull)** — all non-negative combinations of a set of vectors (conic hull), or all non-negative combinations whose weights also sum to 1 (convex hull); the achievable-color cone is the conic hull of the lasso vectors, and a gamut triangle is the convex hull of three primary chromaticities (§2, §6).
- **[Barycentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycentric_coordinate_system)** — the weights expressing a point as a weighted average of a triangle's corners; all positive means inside (D65 white in sRGB: (0.2120, 0.3922, 0.3959)), any negative means outside (§6).
- **[Affine map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_transformation)** — a linear map plus a constant shift, u = Mv + o; RGB → Y′CbCr is affine because of its (16, 128, 128) offset, and is undone by v = M⁻¹(u − o) (§10.3).
- **[Orthonormal basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal_basis) (DCT)** — a basis of unit-length, mutually perpendicular vectors, so the inverse change of basis is just the transpose; JPEG's 64 8×8 DCT cosine patterns form one (§14).
- **[Identity matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_matrix) Id** — the matrix that leaves every vector unchanged (1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere); written Id in the Week 3 notes so it never clashes with the image I (§11.8, §13).

## Week 4 — Great Ideas in Computational Photography
*(full explanations in [`week4-study-notes.md`](./week4-study-notes.md); this is the lookup-speed version)*

- **[Light metering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_meter)** — measuring a scene's overall brightness so the camera can pick an exposure setting; conventionally assumes the frame averages to **18% reflectance** (the "key," the same mid-gray convention as Week 3 §12's gamma example), which is exactly why metering can be "fooled" by scenes far brighter or darker than 18% gray (snow, a coal pile) (§1).
- **[Neutral density (ND) filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral-density_filter)** — darkened glass/film in front of the lens that attenuates all wavelengths roughly equally, reducing light reaching the sensor without shifting color; strength measured in [optical density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_density), OD = log₁₀(1/transmittance) (§3.1).
- **[Radiometric calibration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiometric_calibration)** — measuring, and then undoing, a camera's tone reproduction curve so recorded pixel values are genuinely linear in scene radiance before HDR merging (§5).
- **[Tone reproduction curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transfer_function)** — the nonlinear function *f* a camera silently applies to its raw linear sensor reading before producing a viewable image (e.g. JPEG); a good generic fallback is a power law with γ ≈ 1/2.2 (§5.1, §5.4).
- **[ColorChecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ColorChecker)** — a standardized chart of patches with known reflectance; its bottom row's log-reflectance ladder is one target used to calibrate a camera's tone reproduction curve (§5.3).
- **HDR file formats — [.pfm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netpbm#File_formats), [Radiance .hdr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RGBE_image_format), [OpenEXR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenEXR)** — floating-point image formats needed because merged HDR pixel values span many orders of magnitude and would clip in a fixed-integer format; .hdr's trick is a single 8-bit exponent shared across all three color channels, roughly halving storage versus three independent floats (§6.3).
- **Light probe / [environment map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_mapping)** — an HDR photograph of a mirrored chrome sphere placed in a scene, capturing the entire surrounding illumination in one image; used for image-based relighting of synthetic objects (§6.4).
- **[Tone mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tone_mapping)** — compressing an image's (HDR or LDR) intensity values into the narrower range an actual display can show; progresses from a naive per-channel curve through intensity-only (xyY), edge-aware, and gradient-domain variants to avoid color washout and halo artifacts in turn (§8–§9).
- **[xyY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CIE_1931_color_space#CIE_xyY_color_space)** — a reparameterization of Week 3 §5's CIE xy chromaticity diagram that adds back a luminance axis Y; tonemapping compresses Y alone and leaves xy (chromaticity) untouched, which is what keeps color-aware tonemapping from washing out hue/saturation (§9).
- **[HDR imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_dynamic_range#Capture)** — precisely, producing a *radiometrically linear* image free of over-/under-exposure artifacts, via some combination of a single well-exposed RAW capture, radiometric calibration (§5), and/or bracketing-and-merging multiple exposures (§§3–4); the lecture is explicit that this is **distinct** from tonemapping — HDR imaging compensates for sensor limitations, tonemapping for display limitations — even though consumer "HDR mode" workflows routinely run both together (§10).
- **Coded aperture** — replacing a camera's plain circular aperture stop (or its refractive optics) with a deliberately patterned mask or phase element, chosen so the resulting point spread function's Fourier transform has no exact-zero frequencies ("broadband"), preserving information a plain aperture's blur would destroy (§12, §14).
- **PSF ([point spread function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_spread_function))** — the blob a single point of light forms after passing through an optical system (a disc for a finite pinhole, a circle of confusion for a defocused lens, an Airy pattern at the diffraction limit); for a shift-invariant system, the whole image is the convolution of the ideal sharp image with this kernel. Introduced and defined in full here; Week 5 formalizes it further in the deconvolution/Wiener-filter context (§13.1).
- **[OTF (optical transfer function)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_transfer_function)** — the 2D Fourier transform of the PSF; reports, frequency by frequency, how much of each spatial frequency survives the imaging system. A PSF/OTF is **broadband** when its OTF has no exact zero crossings — equivalently, its convolution matrix has a trivial null space and is, in principle, fully invertible (§13.2, §14.2).
- **Extended depth of field (EDOF)** — an image sharp at every depth simultaneously, produced by deliberately blurring everything with a *depth-invariant*, broadband PSF (via focal sweep or wavefront coding) and then deconvolving with one shared kernel (§15).
- **[Wavefront coding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefront_coding)** — altering a lens's refractive/diffractive optics (rather than attenuating light with a mask) so a point source's rays no longer converge to a sharp focus at any one plane, giving an approximately depth-invariant PSF with no moving parts (§15.2).
- **Focal sweep** — physically moving the sensor or focal plane through a range of positions during one exposure so every scene depth ends up blurred by approximately the same time-integrated kernel, giving a depth-invariant PSF (§15.2–§15.3).
- **Monocular depth estimation** — recovering per-pixel depth from a single ordinary 2D photograph, either from learned pictorial cues (relative size, occlusion, perspective) or from a coded aperture's depth-dependent PSF shape/orientation (§16). *(No precise Wikipedia article covers this specific technique combination — left unlinked.)*
- **[Flutter shutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coded_exposure_photography)** — flickering the shutter open/closed in a coded temporal pattern during one exposure, replacing the ordinary box blur kernel's zero-crossing sinc spectrum with a broadband (zero-free) spectrum, so the resulting motion blur becomes invertible (§18).
- **Parabolic sweep / motion-invariant photography** — moving the camera itself along a parabolic (constantly accelerating) trajectory during exposure so every scene object, regardless of its own velocity, ends up blurred by the same motion-invariant kernel, enabling one shared deconvolution kernel across a whole frame (§19). *(No Wikipedia article exists for this specific technique — left unlinked.)*

## Week 5 — Sampling, Linear Systems, Deconvolution
- **[DFT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_Fourier_transform) / [FFT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_Fourier_transform)** — converts an image from pixel-brightness representation to spatial-frequency representation; FFT is the efficient algorithm for computing it. The underlying idea (image as sum of 2D waves) was introduced early, in Week 1 §12.4, for the hybrid-images homework; this entry covers the discrete, computationally efficient version — the exact DFT formula and why the FFT algorithm is fast.
- **[Aliasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aliasing)** — distortion from sampling below a signal's true frequency content (e.g. moiré).
- **[Nyquist rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyquist_rate)** — the minimum sampling rate needed to avoid aliasing for a given highest frequency present.
- **[Diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffraction)** — bending/spreading of light through small openings — limits how sharply any lens or pinhole can focus.
- **PSF ([point spread function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_spread_function))** — how a single point of light gets smeared by an imaging system; introduced and defined in full in Week 4 §13.1 (coded-aperture imaging) — this week formalizes it further in the deconvolution/Wiener-filter context below.
- **[Wiener filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiener_filter)** — a statistically optimal deconvolution method that accounts for noise.

## Week 6 — Regularized Inverse Problems with ADMM
- **[Inverse problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_problem)** — recovering an unknown true signal from indirect or corrupted measurements.
- **Natural image prior / [regularization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regularization_(mathematics))** — assumptions about what real images typically look like, used to make an ill-posed inverse problem solvable.
- **[ADMM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_direction_method_of_multipliers)** — an optimization algorithm splitting a hard joint problem into easier alternating sub-steps.
- **[Single-pixel imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-pixel_imaging)** — reconstructing a full image from many single-value measurements plus known coded illumination patterns.

## Week 9 — Light Field Imaging
- **[Plenoptic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_field)** — a theoretical function describing light intensity in every direction, at every point in space, wavelength, and time.
- **[Light field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_field)** — a practical capture of many directional rays, often via camera arrays or microlens arrays.
- **[Light field / 3D display](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_field#3D_display)** — a display reproducing different images per viewing angle — one proposed fix for VAC (Week 1, §16).
